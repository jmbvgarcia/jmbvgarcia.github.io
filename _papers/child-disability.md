abstract: >
  Having a child with a severe congenital disability deeply impacts family
  life, yet evidence on the economic effects remains limited. This paper
  examines the impact of a child health shock causing lifelong disability on
  household labor market outcomes in Brazil. Using an exogenous shock to
  disability incidence, the Zika virus epidemic, which caused a surge of
  microcephaly cases in children, we find that mothers of affected children
  reduce labor force participation, including informal work. The shock makes
  the motherhood penalty 75% larger (a 12.0 p.p. reduction compared to 6.8
  p.p. in controls). Informality does not seem to serve as a buffer, as
  informal employment is reduced by an additional 20 p.p. (50%). Brazil's
  disability payment system supplements income but intensifies maternal
  employment losses. Results suggest that child health shocks have persistent
  economic impacts beyond immediate health conditions and that, in developing
  countries, informality and welfare programs shape these impacts.
