# Taller 1 — Discussion guide

**Banerjee & Duflo (2007), "The Economic Lives of the Poor," *JEP* 21(1): 141–167**
Fri 25 Sep 2026 · Bloque 2, 09:50–11:10 · Sala EF802 · Professor-led

*Companion to the deck in `taller01.Rmd`. Structure and notes in English; everything you say out loud is in Spanish, marked »*

---

## What this session has to achieve

This is the **demo session**. The paper matters less than the method. By 11:10 the students should have seen, concretely:

1. What the seven-part reading template looks like when it is filled in.
2. What each of the four roles actually *does* — you play all four, and you name each one out loud as you switch.
3. That a descriptive paper can be excellent and still answer no causal question — and that noticing the gap is the reader's job.

**Say the role out loud every time you switch.** On 23 October ten of them have to do this for real, and the only model they will have is today.

> » *"Ahora me saco el sombrero de Exposición y me pongo el de Escéptico. Fíjense en el cambio: dejo de explicar lo que el artículo dice y empiezo a preguntarme si le creo."*

## Block 1 · Entry ticket (09:50–09:55)

Hand out the template as they write, so no time is lost later.

> » *"A mano, sin apuntes, con su nombre. Cinco minutos. Hoy no lleva nota — quiero que vean el formato antes de que cuente."*

At 09:55, **do not collect them yet**. Ask for answers out loud first — that is what makes the ungraded run worth doing.

> » *"¿Quién me dice qué línea de pobreza usan?"*

### Answer key

**Q1 — the line, and consumption vs. income.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **2** | Names US$1,08/day (extremely poor) and/or US$2,16/day (poor), per capita **consumption**, PPA of 1993 — *and* gives at least one correct reason for consumption |
| **1** | One of the two halves |
| **0** | Neither |

Reasons that earn the point: the self-employed have no accounts and many occupations, so income is close to unmeasurable; income is volatile and lumpy while consumption is smooth, so consumption is a better proxy for permanent income; surveys simply measure consumption better (Deaton 2004).

> » *"Fíjense en que la respuesta correcta tiene dos partes. Decir 'un dólar al día' es la mitad. La otra mitad es por qué consumo y no ingreso — y esa es la parte que se puede preguntar en la PEP."*

**Q2 — a surprising fact plus the authors' explanation.** Full marks require that the explanation be *the one the authors give*, not one the student invented. That distinction is the whole point of the Exposición role, so make it explicit:

> » *"Varios me dieron un hecho y después su propia explicación. Eso es interesante, pero es el rol de Escépticos, no el de Exposición. Exponer es decir qué dice el artículo, aunque uno no le crea."*

## Block 3 · Exposición (10:02–10:12)

The two things that must land:

**(a) It is a JEP paper.** A survey of facts, not a test of a hypothesis.

> » *"¿Qué tipo de artículo es este? No prueba una hipótesis. Reúne hechos. Eso no lo hace peor: lo hace distinto, y hay que leerlo distinto."*

**(b) The identification box is empty, and that is fine.** This is the sentence the whole semester rests on.

> » *"En la plantilla, la casilla 4 va a quedar vacía. No es un error de ustedes ni un defecto del artículo. Es un artículo descriptivo. El problema aparecería si alguien lo citara para decir que X causa Y."*

On Table 1, make one point and move on: the samples are not comparable countries. Udaipur is a poor rural district and Hyderabad is informal urban settlements — **neither represents India**, and the slide's footnote says so. Students will otherwise leave believing "47% of India is extremely poor."

On the two declared assumptions, the second is the interesting one:

> » *"El supuesto de pobreza de largo plazo es cómodo en Udaipur, donde el 86% está bajo US$2,16. ¿Es igual de cómodo en Panamá, donde es el 6%? ¿Quiénes son ese 6%?"*

Expected: the Panamanian 6% may be temporarily poor, or a distinct group (indigenous, rural), so averaging them with Udaipur's 86% compares different populations. That is a real objection, and praising it now sets the tone for the Skeptics block.

**Link to Monday (footnotes 2–3).** The PPA/market exchange-rate ratio correlates with the food share (0,33) and with owning a radio (0,36) — Balassa–Samuelson, seen on Monday, showing up as a measurement problem inside this paper.

> » *"El lunes vimos por qué los precios son más bajos en los países pobres. Aquí ese mismo hecho se convierte en un problema de medición: con el mismo consumo en PPA, lo que realmente se puede comprar cambia entre países."*

## Block 4 · Evidencia (10:12–10:32)

Twenty minutes, four slides. The temptation is to read the bullets; do not. For each of the four facts, **give the number and then ask what it rules out**.

Three moments worth stopping on:

**Alcohol, tobacco and festivals (Hecho 1).** The 30%-more-food line is the paper's most provocative sentence. Ask before commenting:

> » *"¿Es esto un problema?"*

Let the room split. Some will say it is irrational; some will say festivals buy insurance, status and network. Both are on the table — do not resolve it. Note that the paper *also* declines to resolve it, and that this is puzzle 3 for whoever drew it.

**Half the extra spending buys tastier calories, not more calories (Hecho 1).** Flag this now; it is the fact that makes puzzle 3 hard.

**The 57% annual informal interest rate with almost no default (Hecho 4).**

> » *"Casi nadie deja de pagar, y aun así la tasa es de 3,84% mensual, cerca de 57% al año. Si el riesgo de no pago es bajo, ¿qué explica una tasa así?"*

Expected: cost of screening and monitoring, tiny loan sizes with fixed costs, local monopoly power, the lender's own cost of funds. All are Unit 8. Good answers here are worth naming as such.

**Latin America slide.** This is where Chilean students plug in. Keep it short but ask the comparison question, because Wednesday's CASEN session is its setup:

> » *"Miren México: 8,1% en alcohol y tabaco, pero 49,6% en alimentos, por debajo del rango del resto. ¿Por qué América Latina se ve distinta en esta tabla?"*

Expected: higher incomes, so the food share falls (Engel); more urbanised; extremely poor are a smaller and possibly more selected group.

## Block 5 · Escépticos, talk (10:32–10:40)

Eight minutes, two slides. The organizing sentence:

> » *"Hasta aquí les creí los hechos. Ahora quiero ver si les creo las explicaciones. Son cosas distintas."*

Work the three stories for "why don't the poor eat more," then put the question to the room:

> » *"¿Qué dato o qué experimento distinguiría entre las tres?"*

Do not let this run long — it is a rehearsal for the pair activity, not a substitute. Two or three answers and move.

On measurement: the recall-period point is the one students never think of and will need all semester.

> » *"Una encuesta que pregunta por los últimos siete días y otra por los últimos treinta no miden lo mismo, aunque las dos digan 'gasto en alimentos'. Cuando comparen dos países, pregúntense siempre si la pregunta era la misma."*

Close the block with the hardest question in the paper, and say that you do not have an answer:

> » *"'Podrían ahorrar más.' ¿Con qué criterio decimos que una elección ajena es un error? Yo no tengo una respuesta limpia. Guárdenla: vuelve en la Unidad 8."*

## Block 6 · Pair activity (10:40–10:50)

Eight minutes of work (they already have their puzzle), two minutes of setup and circulation.

> » *"Ocho minutos. Tres cosas, por escrito: dos explicaciones rivales, una predicción en la que las dos difieran, y qué dato o experimento las separaría. La predicción es la parte difícil — si sus dos explicaciones predicen exactamente lo mismo, todavía no son rivales."*

Circulate. The failure mode is pairs producing two explanations that are not distinguishable. When you see it:

> » *"Si su explicación A fuera verdadera y la B falsa, ¿qué verían en los datos que no verían al revés?"*

### Model answers — all six

You will not know in advance which pairs report, so here is a usable answer for each. Offer these *after* the pairs speak, not instead of them.

**1 · Poca especialización**
- *Rivals:* (A) risk diversification — no insurance, volatile incomes, so several occupations smooth consumption; (B) barriers to scale — no credit and no land, so no single activity can absorb a full day; (C) no wage jobs exist to absorb them.
- *Diverging prediction:* A says give them **insurance** and they specialize; B says give them **capital** and they specialize, while insurance does nothing.
- *Test:* randomize insurance against a grant of the same value, count occupations a year later. Karlan, Osei, Osei-Akoto & Udry (2014, *QJE*) do close to this in Ghana and find **risk**, not credit, is the binding constraint.

**2 · Tantos emprendedores**
- *Rivals:* (A) genuine opportunity — returns to capital are high at tiny scale and they are constrained; (B) necessity — self-employment is the residual when no one is hiring.
- *Diverging prediction:* A predicts a capital injection raises profits a lot and that they would **turn down** a wage job at the going wage; B predicts they take the wage job immediately.
- *Test:* De Mel, McKenzie & Woodruff (2008) randomize grants to Sri Lankan microenterprises and find very high returns — evidence for A. Against that, Blattman & Dercon's Ethiopian factory-job experiment finds workers quit industrial jobs quickly, which complicates B. Note that the paper's own "median business has no paid employees and almost no assets" points at B.
- *Handoff:* Unit 9.

**3 · No comen más**
- *Rivals:* as on the slide — (A) low nutritional returns at this margin; (B) social comparison sets what counts as enough; (C) self-control.
- *Diverging prediction:* A predicts exogenous extra food does **not** raise earnings; B predicts the threshold moves when the whole village's consumption moves; C predicts demand for commitment devices over food.
- *Test:* randomized supplementation measuring earnings. Note the paper's own killer fact: when food spending rises, half goes to **tastier** calories, not more — which is hard for the pure self-control story.
- *Handoff:* Unit 6 (Dasgupta–Ray capacity curve) is this puzzle formalized. Say so.

**4 · No invierten más en educación**
- *Rivals:* (A) returns really are low because the schools are bad — teachers absent 19% of days, 34,9% of Indian 7–14s cannot read a second-grade paragraph; (B) parents **misperceive** the returns; (C) credit constraints.
- *Diverging prediction:* B is the only one where **information alone** changes enrollment; C is the only one where **cash alone** does; A predicts neither works without quality.
- *Test:* all three exist — Jensen (2010) on information in the Dominican Republic, Progresa on cash, Muralidharan & Sundararaman on teacher incentives.
- *Handoff:* Unit 5, and this is the cleanest illustration in the whole set that a single fact supports three policies with three different price tags.

**5 · No ahorran más**
- *Rivals:* (A) no safe place to store money; (B) present bias; (C) claims from relatives tax visible savings.
- *Diverging prediction:* A predicts a plain account is enough; B predicts people will **pay** for illiquidity; C predicts savings rise more when the account is **private**.
- *Test:* offer the three products and compare take-up and balances.
- ⚠️ **This is Dupas & Robinson (2013), the Week 11 taller paper.** Name the question, do not give the results — the "don't give it away" rule applies from the first session. Say: » *"Este enigma es exactamente el artículo que va a presentar un grupo en la Unidad 8. No les cuento cómo termina."*

**6 · No migran por más tiempo**
- *Rivals:* (A) staying away costs them their claim on land, house or the village safety net; (B) the cost of staying scales with time — no housing, no urban contacts; (C) household obligations; (D) an uncertain urban wage versus a certain rural fallback.
- *Diverging prediction:* B is the only one where a **one-off travel subsidy** extends the stay; D is the only one where a **guaranteed job** does.
- *Test:* Bryan, Chowdhury & Mobarak (2014, *Econometrica*) give a small seasonal travel subsidy in Bangladesh; it induces migration and raises consumption.
- *Handoff:* Unit 9, Harris–Todaro.

### Report-back (10:50–10:56)

Call **three pairs from three different puzzles**. Ask for the *prediction* first, not the explanations — it is the part that separates a good answer from a list.

> » *"No me den las dos explicaciones todavía. Denme la predicción en que difieren, y a ver si adivinamos las explicaciones."*

Then Kenya. The fertilizer experiment is the whole session in one slide: information raised use by 10% and the effect faded; a voucher sold right after harvest worked as well as a 50% subsidy; and the puzzle survived anyway, because most farmers took the fertilizer immediately and stored it without trouble.

> » *"Una encuesta no podía separar 'les falta información' de 'les cuesta ahorrar'. Un experimento sí. Y fíjense en lo honesto del resultado: separó dos explicaciones y el enigma siguió abierto."*

## Block 7 · Conexiones (10:56–11:04)

The map slide is the payoff of the entire session — it converts a descriptive paper into the course's syllabus. Go through it briskly, then land the Chile question:

> » *"El miércoles trabajaron con la CASEN. ¿Cuáles de estos hechos esperarían encontrar entre los hogares pobres chilenos, y cuáles no?"*

Expected, and worth steering toward: **yes** — multiple occupations, tiny businesses without employees, informality, thin insurance. **No** — hunger and BMI under 18,5, child mortality at these rates, no electricity, no school access. **The interesting middle** — savings and formal credit, where Chile has near-universal bank access but heavy consumer debt, which is a different problem, not the absence of the market.

If the room is quiet, name the tension yourself:

> » *"Chile tiene los mercados que a Udaipur le faltan. ¿Significa eso que los problemas de la Parte II del curso no aplican, o que aplican de otra forma?"*

## Back pocket

Questions they are likely to ask, with answers.

**"El artículo es de 2007 con datos de 1988–2005. ¿Sigue siendo cierto?"**
The best question anyone can ask today. Honest answer in two parts: the **levels** are dated — extreme poverty went from 43,4% of the world in 1990 to 10,4% in 2024, the numbers you saw on Monday — but the **texture** has held up: many occupations, businesses with no employees, missing insurance and credit. What has changed most is *where*: extreme poverty is now overwhelmingly Sub-Saharan African. Then turn it around: » *"¿Cómo comprobarían ustedes cuál de los hechos sobrevivió?"*

**"¿Por qué líneas tan raras, US$1,08 y US$2,16?"**
1993 PPA. The World Bank has re-based since to 2011, 2017 and now 2021 PPA; today's lines are US$3,00 and US$8,30. Each re-basing moves the headcount by tens of millions without anyone getting richer or poorer — which is Monday's point about the limits of PPA, arriving one week early.

**"¿No es paternalista decir que gastan mal?"**
Take this seriously and side with the student partly. The paper is careful not to call it a mistake; it presents it as a puzzle. The honest position is that "they could save more" always smuggles in a criterion for when someone else's choice is wrong, and economics has no clean one. Award the point out loud — it is exactly the Escépticos role.

**"Si no identifica nada, ¿por qué lo leemos?"**
Because you cannot design an identification strategy before you know which facts need explaining, and because these six puzzles *are* Units 5 to 9. Also: reading a paper that lacks something is the fastest way to learn to notice when it is missing.

**If nobody read the paper.** A real risk: first taller, ungraded ticket, first week. Every number needed for the pair activity is on the Evidence slides, so the session still works — but say plainly that from 23 October the entry ticket is graded and the Evidence block belongs to students, not to you.

**If discussion dies.** Fall back to the concrete: » *"No me digan si están de acuerdo. Díganme qué número del artículo apoya eso."* Pointing at a specific figure restarts a dead room faster than a better question does.
